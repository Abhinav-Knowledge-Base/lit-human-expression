# The City at Night

The city shifts its skin when the sun goes down.
By day, a flat white glare flattens every street,
stretching the same monotonous light across the town.
But under the dark, it becomes somewhere completely new—
a canvas painted in a hundred quiet hues.

The roads surrender their chaos to the calm.
The trees, which stood frustrated in the midday heat—hiding themselves, shrinking beneath the sun—now sway and sing a song into the cold night air.

Even the sky transforms its character.
In daylight, it burns too bright to look upon;
at night, it feels as though a careful hand worked tirelessly
to decorate the dark with delicate care.

How can we wake to spend our lives in such harsh light,
only to close our eyes when the world becomes this beautiful?

The moon doesn’t shout the way the sun does.
It doesn't strip the color from the buildings,
nor drain the energy from the trees.
It simply arrives, quiet and unassuming,
and everything around it agrees to look softer.
It does not demand. It gives space—
space to grow, to glow, to finally be seen.
It watches quietly, holding a secret truth
the loud, blinding day could never understand.

Maybe that is the cruelty and the beauty of it, both at once:
that these same streets, these same trees, this same tired sky,
can carry so much ugliness under the sun,
and so much grace beneath the stars.

Nothing about the city has changed.
Only the hour. Only the light.
Only how much we are willing to forgive it.

Perhaps the question is not why we wake for the harshness
and sleep through the beauty.
Perhaps we need the daylight’s burn to recognize the beauty at all—
the day teaches us how to survive,
and the night reminds us why it was worth it.
